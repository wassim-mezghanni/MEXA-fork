\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the empirical findings. Section~\ref{sec:results-mexa} gives the primary MEXA alignment scores, starting with the reproduction of reference benchmarks, then evaluating modern causal decoders and comparing with bidirectional encoders and sentence embedding models. Section~\ref{sec:results-moe} examines Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures. Section~\ref{sec:results-non-english-pivots} tests cross-lingual alignment under non-English pivot languages. Section~\ref{sec:results-cross-experiment} analyzes alignment by language family, script, and tokenization fertility. Section~\ref{sec:results-validity} validates MEXA scores against downstream benchmarks.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{MEXA Score Results}
\label{sec:results-mexa}

We evaluate cross-lingual representation alignment using the bidirectional precision-at-1 retrieval metric $\mu C(L_1, L_2, m, l)$ defined in Section~\ref{sec:mexa-pipeline}. Across transformer layers, scores are aggregated using peak alignment ($\mu_{\text{Max}}$) and layer-averaged alignment ($\mu_{\text{Mean}}$). Evaluations are conducted on two distinct parallel corpora: the FLORES-200 benchmark subset (116 language-script pairs overlapping with Belebele, $n = 100$ parallel sentences) and the Bible subset (101 languages, $n = 103$ parallel sentences).

Following standard empirical practice, we first validate the experimental pipeline by reproducing the reference benchmark results from \citet{kargaran2025mexa}. We then present the comprehensive evaluation across 27 publicly available checkpoints representing 16 model architectures across four architectural categories: dense causal decoders, sparse Mixture-of-Experts, masked language models, and dedicated sentence encoders.

On FLORES, Llama~3.1~8B yields $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.6706$ and $\mu_{\text{Mean}} = 0.4143$ across the 116 language-script pairs. Mistral~7B~v0.3 scores $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.4934$ and $\mu_{\text{Mean}} = 0.2813$. Among encoder models, XLM-RoBERTa Large obtains $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.6441$ ($\mu_{\text{Mean}} = 0.4256$) and Glot500 reaches $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.5889$ ($\mu_{\text{Mean}} = 0.3893$). LaBSE, as expected from its contrastive training, scores $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.9510$ and $\mu_{\text{Mean}} = 0.7230$.

To examine these representation dynamics, Figure~\ref{fig:embedding-projections} plots 2D t-SNE projections of FLORES-200 parallel sentence embeddings across the layers of Llama~3.1~8B. At Layer~0, sentences cluster primarily by surface script and lexical features. By Layer~16, parallel sentences across all languages merge into a single shared semantic space regardless of script. At Layer~32, the representations separate again as next-token prediction forces the hidden states into language-specific vocabulary regions. This reproduces the three-phase alignment trajectory reported by \citet{kargaran2025mexa}.

Figure~\ref{fig:max-vs-mean} compares peak alignment ($\mu_{\text{Max}}$) with layer-averaged alignment ($\mu_{\text{Mean}}$) on FLORES. Across all models, the two metrics correlate strongly ($\rho = 0.9500$, $p < 10^{-7}$), and the correlation is tighter among causal decoders ($\rho = 0.9762$, $p < 10^{-4}$). A model's peak score is therefore a reliable predictor of its average layer alignment.

Figure~\ref{fig:flores-vs-bible} compares FLORES alignment scores with Bible scores. On the Bible corpus, scores drop across all models: Llama~3.1~8B falls to $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.4180$, down $25.26$ percentage points from FLORES. Biblical text is archaic and differs substantially from the web text used during pre-training. However, relative rankings remain stable across both corpora ($\rho = 0.9765$, $p < 10^{-10}$), showing that MEXA produces consistent model comparisons across domains.

\subsection{Primary Evaluation and Architectural Hierarchy}
\label{subsec:results-primary-eval}

Table~\ref{tab:mexa-main-results} reports the alignment scores across the primary evaluated models across both FLORES and Bible benchmarks, with overall score distributions shown in Figure~\ref{fig:flores-all-models-barchart}.

Across model families, the results show a consistent ranking. Sentence embedding models (mE5-base and LaBSE) score highest, with peak values above $0.95$ driven by contrastive objectives that explicitly map parallel sentences together. Untuned bidirectional encoders (XLM-RoBERTa Large and Glot500) form a middle tier ($\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.58$--$0.64$), performing on par with mid-sized causal decoders despite lacking contrastive training.

Causal decoders vary widely depending on parameter count, vocabulary size, and pre-training data. Recent large decoders align well on FLORES: Qwen~3.5~9B reaches $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.7794$ and Llama~3.1~8B reaches $0.6706$. Smaller models in the Qwen~3 family (0.6B to 4B) and older baselines like Mistral~7B~v0.3 remain below $0.50$. Parameter scale alone does not guarantee alignment: Apertus models score low on FLORES ($\mu_{\text{Max}} \le 0.4292$), where the 70B variant reaches only $0.3190$, reflecting differences in multilingual pre-training coverage and tokenization efficiency.

\subsection{New Model Families}
\label{subsec:results-new-models}

We extend the evaluation to recent causal decoder families, examining parameter scaling in the Qwen series \citep{yang2025qwen3} and open multilingual architectures from the Swiss AI Apertus project \citep{apertus2025}.

\paragraph{Parameter scaling in Qwen models.}
Across the Qwen~3 family, alignment scores increase consistently with model capacity for both peak ($\mu_{\text{Max}}$) and layer-averaged ($\mu_{\text{Mean}}$) metrics (Figure~\ref{fig:parameter-scaling}). Scaling from 0.6B to 8B parameters more than doubles depth-averaged alignment ($\mu_{\text{Mean}}$ from $0.1426$ to $0.3149$) and raises peak alignment by over $22$ percentage points ($\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.3459 \rightarrow 0.5720$).

The newer Qwen~3.5~9B model achieves the highest alignment among all tested causal decoders on FLORES ($\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.7794$, $\mu_{\text{Mean}} = 0.5517$), outperforming Llama~3.1~8B by over $10$ percentage points in peak alignment and $13$ points in mean alignment. This advantage stems from Qwen's broader multilingual pre-training mixture and a larger, more script-diverse vocabulary.

\paragraph{Swiss AI Apertus models.}
The Apertus model family \citep{apertus2025} shows distinct domain-dependent behavior across datasets. On FLORES, Apertus-Mini~4B (v1.1) achieves $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.5840$, while the larger 8B and 70B variants show lower alignment ($\mu_{\text{Max}} \le 0.4292$). On the Bible corpus, however, Apertus models achieve higher relative alignment (Apertus~8B reaches $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.4242$ and Apertus~1.5~70B reaches $0.5036$). This contrast suggests that Apertus's pre-training data distribution and tokenization provide relatively stronger representation sharing on archaic and lower-resource text, whereas models like Qwen and Llama align more strongly on modern web prose.

\subsection{Encoder and Embedding Models}
\label{subsec:results-encoder-embedding}

A natural question is how general-purpose language models compare with models that are explicitly trained for cross-lingual retrieval. We split non-decoder architectures into three groups: pre-trained bidirectional MLMs, contrastively trained sentence encoders, and decoder-based embedding models. Table~\ref{tab:mexa-main-results} and Figure~\ref{fig:layer-trajectories} report their alignment scores.

\paragraph{Contrastive training closes the gap.}
General decoders are trained only on next-token prediction. Sentence embedding models, by contrast, use contrastive objectives that pull parallel-sentence representations together. The difference is large: dedicated sentence encoders reach near-perfect scores on FLORES (mE5-base reaches $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.9710$ and LaBSE reaches $0.9510$), also outperforming pre-trained bidirectional MLMs like XLM-RoBERTa Large ($0.6441$). The decoder-based Qwen~3 Embedding series also scores well: $0.7069$ at 0.6B, $0.8033$ at 4B, and $0.8465$ at 8B. Even the 0.6B embedding variant outperforms the 8B causal base model ($0.7069$ vs.\ $0.5720$), which shows that specialized contrastive training strongly promotes representation alignment, even though generative decoders serve a different primary objective.

\paragraph{Peak vs.\ sustained alignment.}
Although mE5-base has a slightly higher peak than LaBSE ($0.9710$ vs.\ $0.9510$), LaBSE maintains much higher alignment across all intermediate layers ($\mu_{\text{Mean}} = 0.7230$ vs.\ $0.5373$). As seen in Figure~\ref{fig:layer-trajectories}, alignment in general MLMs (XLM-RoBERTa, Glot500, mmBERT) is concentrated in a few middle-to-late layers. LaBSE, by contrast, is aligned from the earliest transformer blocks onward, consistent with its architecture being designed specifically for zero-shot sentence retrieval.

\subsection{Sample Size and Language Coverage}
\label{subsec:results-sample-size-coverage}

Evaluating representation alignment requires making two choices: how many parallel sentences to evaluate per language ($n$), and how many language varieties to include. The standard benchmark subset uses $n = 100$ sentences across 116 language-script pairs on FLORES-200 (the subset overlapping with Belebele) and $n = 103$ verses across 101 languages on the Bible corpus. We evaluate two expansions to test how these choices influence retrieval scores, with results summarized in Table~\ref{tab:subset-vs-full}.

First, increasing sample size from $n = 100$ to $n = 1{,}012$ on FLORES (holding the 116 Belebele-overlapping language-script pairs fixed) expands the pool, raising distractor density during Precision@1 retrieval. Under this harder criterion, absolute scores decrease across all models. For example, peak alignment in Qwen~3.5~9B drops from $0.7794$ to $0.7167$, while Qwen~3~4B drops from $0.4382$ to $0.3446$. In contrast, encoders experience much smaller reductions (mE5-base drops from $0.9710$ to $0.9501$). Despite these lower absolute values, relative model rankings remain identical: the rank correlation between the 100-sentence and 1,012-sentence runs is Spearman $\rho = 0.9956$ for peak alignment ($\mu_{\text{Max}}$) and $\rho = 1.0000$ for mean alignment ($\mu_{\text{Mean}}$).

Second, expanding evaluation to the full language inventory introduces the low-resource tail. Evaluating all 204 FLORES language-script pairs (evaluating 203 non-English target varieties against the English pivot) reduces scores further (Qwen~3.5~9B drops to $0.5881$, and Qwen~3~1.7B drops to $0.3008$). Expanding the Bible corpus from 101 languages to the full inventory of 1,401 language-script pairs causes a steep decline across all generative decoders, where peak scores fall below $0.10$. These extreme tail languages feature non-Latin scripts and low web representation, causing heavy subword fragmentation during tokenization. However, across the overlapping language subsets, relative per-language difficulty rankings correlate strongly ($\rho = 1.0000$). These results confirm that while absolute MEXA values depend on distractor pool size and language resource tier, relative comparisons across architectures remain invariant.

\section{Mixture-of-Experts Models}
\label{sec:results-moe}

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures decouple total model capacity from inference cost by routing each token to a subset of feed-forward experts. A key question is whether this routing isolates languages into separate experts and fragments the representation space, or whether experts specialize by function across languages.

\paragraph{Dense vs.\ sparse alignment.}
Figure~\ref{fig:moe-layer-trajectories} compares layer-wise alignment trajectories for dense and sparse models on FLORES-200 across the Mistral/Mixtral (left) and Gemma (right) families.

In the Mistral family, the dense baseline Mistral~7B~v0.3 scores $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.4934$ and $\mu_{\text{Mean}} = 0.2813$. The sparse Mixtral~8x7B reaches $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.5385$ ($\mu_{\text{Mean}} = 0.3214$), while Mixtral~8x22B reaches $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.6120$ ($\mu_{\text{Mean}} = 0.3950$). These improvements stem from larger total parameter capacity rather than per-token compute: although Mixtral models activate 13B and 39B parameters per token, their total capacities are 47B and 141B parameters. In the Gemma family, Gemma~4~26B-A4B (4B active parameters) maintains high alignment through deep layers, matching dense baselines with similar active parameter counts. Its shared expert pathway helps sustain this alignment in late layers.

\paragraph{Routing and the shared representation space.}
Figure~\ref{fig:moe-router-distributions} shows a conceptual schematic of expert gating across network depth, based on routing patterns documented in the MoE literature \citep{kudugunta2021beyond,fedus2022switch,jiang2024mixtral}.

In sparse architectures like Mixtral~8x7B, top-$k$ gating operates on individual tokens rather than entire languages. At Layer~0, differences in surface script and tokenization cause minor routing variations across languages. By Layer~15, representations merge into a shared semantic space, resulting in near-uniform expert utilization ($\approx 12.5\%$ per expert across all 8 experts). In Layer~31, routing redistributes slightly to support language-specific vocabulary prediction. Because experts specialize by syntactic and semantic function rather than by language, sparse scaling expands model capacity without breaking cross-lingual alignment.

\paragraph{Raw alignment across alternative pivots.}
Under standard uncentered scoring, non-English pivots match or exceed English alignment across modern decoders. On Llama~3.1~8B, French, German, and Arabic pivots all produce higher peak alignment than English ($\mu_{\text{Max}} \approx 0.73$ vs.\ $0.6735$). On Qwen~3.5~9B, alignment remains consistently high across all six pivots ($\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.73$--$0.80$). Language difficulty rankings correlate strongly across all pivot pairs ($\rho > 0.95$ for major pivots), showing that relative language performance is independent of the reference anchor.

\paragraph{Mean-centering eliminates pivot variance.}
Removing language-specific centroid vectors via mean-centering (Equation~\ref{eq:mean-centering}) causes alignment scores to rise and converge across all pivots, including English. For Qwen~3.5~9B, peak alignment across all six pivots clusters in a narrow band between $0.9443$ and $0.9564$ (English reaches $0.9564$, German $0.9547$, Arabic $0.9485$, and Basque $0.9444$). On Llama~3.1~8B, scores similarly converge to $\mu_{\text{Max}} \approx 0.88$--$0.90$.

\paragraph{Implications for multilingual representations.}
These results demonstrate that multilingual LLMs do not rely on English as a mandatory internal hub. Furthermore, even a language isolate like Basque can serve as an effective pivot in well-trained models: Qwen~3.5~9B reaches $\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.7995$ uncentered and $0.9444$ centered with Basque. Lower Basque performance in Mistral~7B~v0.3 ($0.2158$ uncentered) stems from limited training data for low-resource languages rather than a structural reliance on English. Once language-specific centroid offsets are subtracted, representations occupy a shared, multi-directional space where any well-trained language functions as a pivot.

\section{Cross-Experiment Language Analysis}
\label{sec:results-cross-experiment}

\paragraph{Script and resource tier.}
Alignment scores vary substantially across script families and resource levels, as reflected in Figure~\ref{fig:fertility-vs-alignment}. Across all causal decoders, high-resource Romance and Germanic languages written in Latin script (e.g., French, Spanish, German) reach the highest alignment ($\mu_{\text{Max}} \ge 0.75$). High-resource non-Latin scripts (Cyrillic, Devanagari, Arabic, Han) achieve moderate-to-high alignment ($\mu_{\text{Max}} \approx 0.60$--$0.75$), whereas low-resource non-Latin scripts (including Ge'ez for Amharic, Myanmar for Burmese, Khmer, and Cherokee) score lowest ($\mu_{\text{Max}} \approx 0.25$--$0.45$). FLORES-200 contains an uneven distribution across these groups: Latin-script language-script pairs comprise the majority of the dataset (126 of 204 pairs), while low-resource non-Latin scripts are represented by far fewer varieties.

\paragraph{The tokenizer fertility tax.}
A primary factor behind the low alignment of under-represented languages is subword tokenization fertility $F_L$, the average number of subword tokens produced per word \citep{petrov2023tokenizer}. On a logarithmic scale (Figure~\ref{fig:fertility-vs-alignment}), alignment exhibits a strong negative correlation with fertility (log-linear Pearson $r = -0.56$, Spearman $\rho = -0.67$, $p < 10^{-17}$). This negative relationship holds within individual script families as well: Latin ($r = -0.76$, $n = 126$), Arabic ($r = -0.88$, $n = 22$), Cyrillic ($r = -0.93$, $n = 12$), and Devanagari ($r = -0.71$, $n = 10$).

Figure~\ref{fig:tokenization-comparison} illustrates the underlying mechanism. When an under-represented language or script (such as Minangkabau in Arabic script) passes through a standard tokenizer, words are split into short character fragments or byte tokens. Under causal attention, these short pieces contain less semantic information than the complete word-level tokens available in high-resource languages. Furthermore, position-weighted pooling (Equation~\ref{eq:weighted-avg}) spreads attention across a longer sequence length $T$, reducing the relative weight $w_t$ of each token and adding noise to the pooled embedding $e^{(l)}$. As diagonal similarity $c_{ii}(l)$ decreases relative to off-diagonal distractors, retrieval accuracy declines.

\section{Validity of MEXA}
\label{sec:results-validity}

To check whether the internal alignment measured by MEXA actually predicts downstream performance, we follow the validation methodology of \citet{kargaran2025mexa} and evaluate MEXA scores against two multilingual benchmarks: Belebele reading comprehension \citep{bandarkar2024belebele} and m-ARC scientific reasoning \citep{lai2023okapi}, at both the model level (Figure~\ref{fig:validity-model-level}) and the language level (Figure~\ref{fig:validity-language-level}).

\paragraph{Correlation with Belebele reading comprehension.}
Model-level MEXA scores on FLORES correlate with accuracy on the Belebele reading comprehension benchmark \citep{bandarkar2024belebele}. In the reference evaluations of \citet{kargaran2025mexa}, this correlation is strong ($\rho = 0.8420$, $p < 10^{-5}$). Across the six models evaluated in this thesis (five Qwen variants and Apertus~8B), the Spearman rank correlation is $\rho = 0.771$ (Pearson $r = 0.754$), as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:validity-model-level}. Within the Qwen~3 scaling family alone (0.6B to 9B), the ranking is monotonically preserved: higher MEXA alignment consistently predicts higher downstream accuracy.

\paragraph{The Apertus outlier.}
The correlation is attenuated by Apertus~8B, which achieves relatively high downstream accuracy (Belebele $0.627$, m-ARC $0.429$) despite low MEXA alignment ($\mu_{\text{Max}} = 0.3817$). This suggests that Apertus organizes its multilingual representations in a geometry that cosine-based retrieval does not fully capture possibly due to higher anisotropy or a non-isotropic shared space that still supports effective classification. Excluding Apertus, the Qwen-only subset yields $\rho = 0.900$ for both benchmarks, with a single rank inversion between Qwen~3 1.7B and Qwen~3 4B: the smaller model achieves slightly higher MEXA ($0.490$ vs.\ $0.438$) but lower downstream accuracy on both tasks.

\paragraph{Correlation with m-ARC reasoning.}
The correlation with m-ARC \citep{lai2023okapi} follows a similar pattern ($\rho = 0.8105$, $p < 10^{-4}$ in reference studies; $\rho = 0.600$, Pearson $r = 0.582$ in Figure~\ref{fig:validity-model-level}). The lower value again reflects the Apertus outlier; restricting to Qwen models yields $\rho = 0.900$, with the same single rank inversion between Qwen~3 1.7B and 4B noted above. These results confirm that MEXA's training-free retrieval criterion based on parallel sentence pairs remains a reliable proxy for multilingual reasoning performance within architectures that share similar representational strategies, while also revealing that cross-architecture comparisons require caution when models differ substantially in their internal geometry.

\paragraph{Language-level downstream validity.}
At the language level, Figure~\ref{fig:validity-language-level} evaluates per-language MEXA scores against Belebele accuracy for Llama~3.1~8B across 110 languages, showing a strong positive correlation ($r = 0.991$, $\rho = 0.986$, $p < 10^{-80}$). Languages with higher internal representation alignment achieve consistently higher downstream comprehension accuracy.
