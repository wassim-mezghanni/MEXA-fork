\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) now drive much of natural language processing, from question answering to code generation \citep{brown2020gpt3}. A growing number of these models, including Llama~3.1 \citep{dubey2024llama3}, Qwen~3 \citep{yang2025qwen3}, and Mistral \citep{jiang2023mistral}, are trained on multilingual web-scale corpora and can perform tasks in languages they were never explicitly supervised on. However , this zero-shot cross-lingual transfer is unevenly distributed. A model that appears fluent in French or German may fail on Amharic or Burmese, and we still do not fully understand what enables or prevents transfer across the world's 7,000+ languages.

One explanation is the \emph{pivot hypothesis}: during pre-training, multilingual transformers develop a shared, language-agnostic representation space (an \emph{interlingua}) in their intermediate hidden layers, where semantically equivalent sentences from different languages end up near each other \citep{wendler2024llamas}. If this is true, internal representational alignment should predict cross-lingual downstream performance without any task-specific evaluation. Testing the claim, however, requires a metric that can probe representations directly, across hundreds of languages, at every layer of a deep network.

MEXA (Multilingual Evaluation via Cross-Lingual Alignment) \citep{kargaran2025mexa} is such a metric. Given parallel sentences in a target and a pivot language, MEXA extracts layer-wise hidden states, computes cosine similarity matrices, and applies a bidirectional precision-at-1 retrieval criterion that handles the severe anisotropy of LLM embedding spaces \citep{ethayarajh2019contextualized, hammerl2023anisotropy}. The output is a single score per language per layer. No generation, fine-tuning, or reference translations are needed.

This thesis reproduces the original MEXA evaluation and extends it along four axes: new decoder families and scales, sparse mixture-of-experts architectures, encoder and embedding models, and non-English pivot languages. The goal is to map out where and how modern LLMs achieve (or fail to achieve) cross-lingual alignment.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-motivation}

\paragraph{The multilingual promise and its gaps.}
LLMs are increasingly deployed in multilingual settings, from machine translation to multilingual search and document understanding. Yet standard evaluation benchmarks cover only a fraction of the world's languages and scripts \citep{bandarkar2024belebele, lai2023okapi}. Languages written in non-Latin scripts or lacking large digital corpora are systematically under-served: their tokenizers fragment words into character-level pieces (the \emph{fertility tax}), their representations are poorly aligned with the English-centric core, and their downstream performance lags far behind \citep{nllbteam2022}. Understanding where this gap originates (at the tokenizer, in the representation space, or in the training data) requires tools that can diagnose model internals rather than just measure task output.

\paragraph{The need for reference-free evaluation.}
Traditional multilingual evaluation relies on downstream benchmarks that mix representation quality with generation ability and prompt sensitivity . A model may score well on a translated reading comprehension test not because its representations are aligned, but because it memorized surface patterns. The reverse is also possible: a model with strong internal alignment may underperform due to poor decoding. Reference-free metrics like MEXA sidestep these issues by probing hidden-state geometry directly, giving a cleaner signal for comparing architectures, training recipes, and scaling behavior.

\paragraph{Rapid architectural change.}
Since the publication of MEXA, several major model families have appeared: Qwen~3 and~3.5 \citep{yang2025qwen3}, Mixtral \citep{jiang2024mixtral}, and Gemma~4, each with different training data compositions, vocabulary sizes, and architectural choices such as sparse mixture-of-experts routing. Whether these models improve or degrade multilingual alignment is an empirical question the original study could not address. This thesis fills that gap.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro-research-questions}

This thesis investigates four research questions, each corresponding to one experimental axis:

\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1 (Scale \& Recipe)] \emph{How does cross-lingual alignment scale with model parameters and pre-training recipe?} \\
    We evaluate decoder families across parameter sweeps (Qwen~3 at 0.6B--8B, Gemma~4 at 2B--31B) and compare alignment across families at matched scale (Llama~3.1~8B vs.\ Qwen~3~8B vs.\ Apertus~8B) to separate the effects of scale, vocabulary design, and multilingual data composition.

    \item[RQ2 (Sparse Architectures)] \emph{Does sparse mixture-of-experts routing fragment or preserve the shared interlingua?} \\
    We compare dense models against their MoE counterparts (Mistral~7B vs.\ Mixtral~8x7B and~8x22B; dense Gemma~4 vs.\ Gemma~4~26B-A4B) to test whether expert specialization leads to language-specific routing that degrades the shared representation space.

    \item[RQ3 (Pivot Independence)] \emph{Is the interlingua English-centric or genuinely language-agnostic?} \\
    We replace the English pivot with French, German, Arabic, Chinese, and Basque (spanning high-resource Latin-script, non-Latin-script, and a language isolate) and apply mean-centering to remove language-identity offsets, testing whether alignment scores hold regardless of pivot choice.

    \item[RQ4 (Predictive Validity)] \emph{Does internal representational alignment predict downstream multilingual task performance?} \\
    We correlate MEXA scores with Belebele reading comprehension \citep{bandarkar2024belebele} and m-ARC reasoning \citep{lai2023okapi} at both the model level and the language level to test whether MEXA is a reliable proxy for functional multilingual capability.
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reproduction and validation.} We reproduce the original MEXA evaluation on Llama~3.1~8B and Mistral~7B~v0.3 with matching configurations, confirm pipeline correctness, and use the reproduced scores as a verified baseline for all extensions.

    \item \textbf{Extended model coverage.} We apply MEXA to over 25 models in four architectural categories: causal decoders (Qwen~3, Qwen~3.5, Apertus, Gemma~4), sparse MoE models (Mixtral~8x7B/8x22B, Gemma~4~26B-A4B), masked-LM encoders (XLM-RoBERTa, Glot500 \citep{imani2023glot500}), and contrastive sentence encoders (LaBSE \citep{feng2022labse}, mE5 \citep{wang2024multilingual}). To our knowledge, this is the broadest cross-architectural alignment comparison available.

    \item \textbf{Non-English pivot analysis.} We show that MEXA alignment is not an artifact of English centricity. Substituting five typologically diverse pivot languages yields consistent scores, and mean-centering raises peak alignment to $\mu_{\text{Max}} \ge 0.89$ across all pivots, pointing to a genuinely multilingual interlingua.

    \item \textbf{Tokenizer fertility and script analysis.} We quantify the fertility tax across all evaluated models and corpora, find strong negative correlations ($r \in [-0.46, -0.62]$) between subword fragmentation and alignment, and identify script family as the primary grouping variable for per-language performance.

    \item \textbf{Open tooling and visualization.} We develop an interactive React dashboard for exploring alignment scores, layer trajectories, fertility correlations, and embedding projections across all models, languages, and configurations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:intro-thesis-outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows:

\begin{description}
    \item[Chapter~\ref{ch:background}: Background] reviews multilingual transformer architectures, the tokenization bottleneck, cross-lingual transfer, the English pivot assumption, evaluation paradigms (reference-based vs.\ reference-free), alignment challenges such as anisotropy, and the parallel corpora used in this work (FLORES-200 and the Parallel Bible Corpus).

    \item[Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}: Methodology] formalizes the four-stage MEXA pipeline (embedding extraction, similarity computation, bidirectional retrieval scoring, and layer-wise aggregation) and its extensions (mean-centering, non-English pivots). It then describes the 25+ evaluated models, dataset configurations, analysis methods at four levels (model, language, layer, and validity), and the compute infrastructure.

    \item[Chapter~\ref{ch:results}: Results and Discussion] reports the empirical findings, starting with the reproduction check and model-family comparisons, then covering scaling within decoder families, MoE alignment and router analysis, non-English pivot experiments, per-language analysis including the tokenizer fertility tax, and downstream validity correlations with Belebele and m-ARC.

    \item[Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}: Conclusion] summarizes the findings, discusses limitations, and proposes directions for future work.
\end{description}
