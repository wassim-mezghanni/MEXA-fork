\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) now drive much of natural language processing, from question answering to code generation \citep{brown2020gpt3}. A growing number of these models, including Llama~3.1 \citep{dubey2024llama3}, Qwen~3 \citep{yang2025qwen3}, and Mistral \citep{jiang2023mistral}, are trained on multilingual web-scale corpora and can perform tasks in languages they were never explicitly supervised on. However, this zero-shot cross-lingual transfer is unevenly distributed. A model that performs well in French or German may still fail on languages like Amharic or Burmese, where scarce training data and non-Latin scripts (Ge'ez and Myanmar) cause heavy subword fragmentation. We still do not fully understand what enables or prevents transfer across the world's 7,000+ languages.

One explanation is the \emph{pivot hypothesis} \citep{wendler2024llamas}: during pre-training, multilingual models appear to route different languages through a shared internal representation space (an \emph{interlingua}) in their middle layers. In English-centric models, this space often acts like an internal pivot, mapping inputs from various languages onto shared semantic concepts before decoding back into the target language. If such an interlingua exists, the degree of geometric alignment between parallel sentences inside the network should reflect how well the model understands those languages. Testing this idea across hundreds of languages, however, requires a method that directly probes these internal representations layer by layer.

MEXA (Multilingual Evaluation via Cross-Lingual Alignment) \citep{kargaran2025mexa} was introduced for this purpose. Instead of evaluating downstream task outputs or generating text, MEXA directly measures how accurately a model's internal hidden states match parallel sentences between a target language and a pivot language. This produces a layer-by-layer alignment score for any language without requiring task prompts, text generation, or downstream fine-tuning, operating directly on parallel sentence pairs.

This thesis reproduces the original MEXA evaluation and extends it along four axes: new decoder families and model sizes, sparse mixture-of-experts architectures, encoder, token-level, and sentence-level models, and non-English pivot languages. The goal is to map out where and how modern LLMs achieve (or fail to achieve) cross-lingual alignment.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-motivation}

\paragraph{The multilingual promise and its gaps.}
LLMs are increasingly deployed in multilingual settings, from machine translation to multilingual search and document understanding. Yet standard evaluation benchmarks cover only a fraction of the world's languages and scripts \citep{bandarkar2024belebele, lai2023okapi}. Languages written in non-Latin scripts or lacking large digital corpora are systematically under-served: their tokenizers fragment words into character-level pieces (the \emph{fertility tax}, where high subword-to-word ratios inflate sequence length and dilute semantic content per token \citep{petrov2023tokenizer}), their representations are poorly aligned with the English-centric core, and their downstream performance lags far behind \citep{nllbteam2022}. Understanding where this gap originates (at the tokenizer, in the representation space, or in the training data) requires tools that can diagnose model internals rather than just measure task output.

\paragraph{The need for training-free, task-independent evaluation.}
Traditional multilingual evaluation relies on downstream benchmarks that mix representation quality with generation ability and prompt sensitivity \citep{bandarkar2024belebele, lai2023okapi, kargaran2025mexa}. A model may score well on a translated reading comprehension test not because its representations are aligned, but because it memorized surface patterns. The reverse is also possible: a model with strong internal alignment may underperform due to poor decoding. Training-free alignment metrics based on parallel sentence pairs such as MEXA sidestep these issues by probing hidden-state geometry directly without requiring text generation or fine-tuning, giving a cleaner signal for comparing architectures, training recipes, and scaling behavior.

\paragraph{Rapid architectural change.}
Since the publication of MEXA, several major model families have appeared: Qwen~3 and~3.5 \citep{yang2025qwen3}, Mixtral \citep{jiang2024mixtral}, and Gemma~4, each with different training data compositions, vocabulary sizes, and architectural choices such as sparse mixture-of-experts routing. Whether these models improve or degrade multilingual alignment is an empirical question the original study could not address. This thesis fills that gap.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro-research-questions}

This thesis investigates four research questions, each corresponding to one experimental axis:

\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1 (Scale \& Recipe)] \emph{How does cross-lingual alignment scale with model parameters and pre-training recipe?} \\
    We evaluate multiple decoder families (Llama~3.1, Qwen~3/3.5, Mistral, Gemma~4, and Apertus) across parameter sweeps (e.g., Qwen~3 from 0.6B to 8B and Gemma~4 from 2B to 31B) and compare alignment across families at matched scale (such as Llama~3.1~8B vs.\ Qwen~3~8B vs.\ Apertus~8B) to separate the effects of parameter scale, vocabulary design, and multilingual training mixture.

    \item[RQ2 (Sparse Architectures)] \emph{Does sparse mixture-of-experts routing fragment or preserve the shared interlingua?} \\
    We compare dense models against their MoE counterparts (Mistral~7B vs.\ Mixtral~8x7B and~8x22B; dense Gemma~4 vs.\ Gemma~4~26B-A4B) to test whether expert specialization leads to language-specific routing that degrades the shared representation space.

    \item[RQ3 (Pivot Independence)] \emph{Is the interlingua English-centric or genuinely language-agnostic?} \\
    We replace the English pivot with French, German, Arabic, Chinese, and Basque (spanning high-resource Latin-script, non-Latin-script, and a language isolate) and apply mean-centering to remove language-identity offsets, testing whether alignment scores hold regardless of pivot choice.

    \item[RQ4 (Predictive Validity)] \emph{Does internal representational alignment predict downstream multilingual task performance?} \\
    Following the validation methodology introduced by \citet{kargaran2025mexa}, we correlate MEXA scores with Belebele reading comprehension \citep{bandarkar2024belebele} and m-ARC reasoning \citep{lai2023okapi} at both the model level and the language level, testing whether this predictive relationship holds across our broader set of newly evaluated architectures.
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reproduction and validation.} We reproduce the original MEXA evaluation on Llama~3.1~8B and Mistral~7B~v0.3 with matching configurations, confirm pipeline correctness, and use the reproduced scores as a verified baseline for all extensions.

    \item \textbf{Extended model coverage.} We evaluate 27 publicly available checkpoints representing 16 model architectures across four architectural categories: dense causal decoders (Qwen~3/3.5, Apertus, Gemma~4, Llama~3.1, Mistral), sparse MoE models (Mixtral~8x7B/8x22B, Gemma~4~26B-A4B), masked language models (XLM-RoBERTa, Glot500 \citep{imani2023glot500}, mmBERT), and dedicated contrastive sentence encoders (LaBSE \citep{feng2022labse}, mE5 \citep{wang2024multilingual}, Qwen~3 Embedding). To our knowledge, this is the broadest cross-architectural alignment comparison available.

    \item \textbf{Non-English pivot analysis.} We show that MEXA alignment is not an artifact of English centricity. Substituting five typologically diverse pivot languages yields consistent scores, and removing language-identity offsets through mean-centering substantially improves alignment across all pivots, pointing to a shared multilingual interlingua.

    \item \textbf{Tokenizer fertility and script analysis.} We quantify the fertility tax across all evaluated models and corpora, find strong negative correlations ($r \in [-0.46, -0.62]$) between subword fragmentation and alignment, and identify script family as the primary grouping variable for per-language performance.

    \item \textbf{Open tooling and reproducibility.} We develop an interactive React dashboard for exploring alignment scores, layer trajectories, fertility correlations, and embedding projections across all models, languages, and configurations. All implementation code, extraction scripts, and evaluation pipelines are publicly available.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/wassim-mezghanni/Evaluating-Multilingual-LLM-Performance-with-Cross-Lingual-Alignment}}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:intro-thesis-outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows:

\begin{description}
    \item[Chapter~\ref{ch:background}: Background] covers multilingual transformer architectures, tokenization bottlenecks, and cross-lingual transfer through internal pivot spaces. It also discusses evaluation methods, contrasting downstream task benchmarks with training-free alignment metrics based on parallel sentence pairs and explaining geometric issues like anisotropy. Finally, it introduces the two parallel corpora used throughout this work: FLORES-200 and the Super-Parallel Bible Corpus.

    \item[Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}: Methodology] formalizes the four-stage MEXA pipeline (embedding extraction, similarity computation, bidirectional retrieval scoring, and layer-wise aggregation) and its extensions (mean-centering, non-English pivots). It then describes the 27 evaluated model checkpoints representing 16 architectures, dataset configurations, analysis methods at four levels (model, language, layer, and validity), and the compute infrastructure.

    \item[Chapter~\ref{ch:results}: Results and Discussion] reports the empirical findings, starting with the reproduction check and model-family comparisons, then covering scaling within decoder families, MoE alignment and router analysis, non-English pivot experiments, per-language analysis including the tokenizer fertility tax, and downstream validity correlations with Belebele and m-ARC.

    \item[Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}: Conclusion] summarizes the findings, discusses limitations, and proposes directions for future work.
\end{description}
