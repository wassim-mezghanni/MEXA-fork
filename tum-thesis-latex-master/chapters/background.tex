\section{Multilingual Language Models}
\label{sec:multilingual-lm}

\subsection{Transformer Architectures}
\label{subsec:architectures-pretraining}

All models evaluated in this thesis are built on the transformer architecture \citep{vaswani2017attention}. A transformer stacks $L$ identical layers, each consisting of a multi-head self-attention block followed by a position-wise feed-forward network. The input to the model is a sequence of tokens, each mapped to a $d$-dimensional vector by an embedding table (layer 0). At every subsequent layer $l$, the self-attention mechanism lets each token attend to other tokens in the sequence, and the resulting hidden states $h_t^{(l)}$ encode progressively more abstract representations of meaning. The MEXA pipeline (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}) extracts exactly these per-layer hidden states.

Multilingual models differ in their architecture (encoder vs.\ decoder) and training objectives (generative vs.\ contrastive), falling into three broad categories in our evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:transformer-schematic}):

\paragraph{Masked language models (MLMs).}
Bidirectional encoders such as XLM-RoBERTa \citep{conneau2020xlmr} and Glot500 \citep{imani2023glot500} use full (bidirectional) attention: every token can attend to every other token in the sequence. They are trained by masking a fraction of input tokens and predicting the masked ones from context. Because attention is bidirectional, each hidden state $h_t^{(l)}$ reflects the full sentence, regardless of position.

\paragraph{Causal decoders.}
Autoregressive models such as Llama~3.1 \citep{dubey2024llama3}, Qwen~3 \citep{yang2025qwen3}, and Apertus \citep{apertus2025} apply a causal attention mask, allowing each token $t$ to attend only to previous positions ($\le t$) to predict the next token. Consequently, early tokens in a sentence carry little context, while the final token has attended to the entire sequence. This asymmetry directly influences how hidden states are pooled into sentence embeddings (Section~\ref{sec:mexa-pipeline}).

\paragraph{Contrastive sentence encoders.}
Sentence-level models such as LaBSE \citep{feng2022labse} and mE5-base \citep{wang2024multilingual} start from a pretrained encoder or decoder but are then fine-tuned with a contrastive loss: parallel sentences (translations) are pulled together in vector space, while non-parallel sentences are pushed apart. The result is an embedding space where cosine similarity between translations is high by design. These models set an upper bound on what alignment looks like when it is an explicit training objective.

\subsection{Sparse Mixture-of-Experts}
\label{subsec:moe-background}

A recent trend in scaling language models is sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) \citep{jiang2024mixtral}. In a standard (dense) transformer, every token passes through the same feed-forward network at each layer. In an MoE transformer, the feed-forward block is replaced by $E$ parallel expert networks, and a learned router selects a small subset (typically $k=2$ out of $E=8$ experts) for each token (Figure~\ref{fig:moe-architecture}). The total parameter count is much larger than in a dense model, but the per-token compute stays roughly constant.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig_moe_architecture.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) transformer architecture}. Left: stacked transformer blocks alternating between multi-head self-attention and MoE layers. Right: internal structure of an MoE layer, where a learned router dynamically directs token representations to top-$k$ routed experts (out of $E$ total), combining their outputs alongside optional shared experts.}
\label{fig:moe-architecture}
\end{figure}

For multilingual alignment, MoE raises a specific question: does the router send tokens from different languages to different experts, or do experts specialize by syntactic or semantic function regardless of language? If experts are language-specific, the shared representation space could fragment. If they are function-specific, alignment should be preserved. We test this empirically in Section~\ref{sec:results-moe}.

\subsection{The Tokenization Bottleneck}
\label{subsec:tokenization-bottleneck}

Before any text reaches the transformer, it must be split into subword tokens. The standard approach is Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) \citep{sennrich2016bpe}: starting from individual bytes or characters, the algorithm iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token until a fixed vocabulary size is reached. Because the merge statistics are computed over the training corpus, languages that are well-represented get longer, more meaningful tokens (e.g., ``scientist'' stays as one token in English), while under-represented languages are split into short, often meaningless fragments (e.g., an Amharic word may become 8+ byte-level tokens).

The ratio of tokens to words is called \emph{fertility} ($F_L$). High fertility has two consequences for cross-lingual alignment \citep{petrov2023tokenizer}. First, the sequence gets longer, so each token's weight in a pooled sentence embedding is diluted. Second, the individual token representations carry less semantic information, because each fragment sees less meaningful context under causal attention. In practice, fertility varies widely across languages: English and German typically require only 1.0 to 1.5 tokens per word, whereas non-Latin scripts like Amharic (Ge'ez) or Burmese (Myanmar) often split words into 4 to 8 or more pieces. Section~\ref{sec:results-cross-experiment} examines how this fragmentation correlates with lower alignment across more than 200 languages and script families.

\subsection{Cross-Lingual Transfer}
\label{subsec:crosslingual-transfer}

Cross-lingual transfer is the ability of a model to perform a task in a target language $L_1$ after being trained primarily in a source language $L_2$ (typically English). For this to work, the model's internal representations must be similar for sentences with the same meaning, regardless of language.

The pivot hypothesis \citep{wendler2024llamas} offers an explanation: multilingual transformers develop a shared \emph{concept space} in their intermediate layers, mapping semantically equivalent sentences to nearby points. This predicts a clear pattern across depth: early layers encode surface features like script and word order, middle layers converge into this shared space, and late layers diverge to generate language-specific tokens.

In fact, \citet{wendler2024llamas} found that for models like Llama-2, this middle space is largely English-centric, with non-English inputs routed through English-like representations. Whether this bias holds across newer architectures and non-English pivot evaluations is a core focus of this thesis (RQ3, Section~\ref{sec:intro-research-questions}).

\subsection{English as a Pivot Language}
\label{subsec:english-pivot}

English dominates both the pre-training data and the parallel data used for evaluation. Most multilingual benchmarks measure performance relative to English, and most alignment metrics (including MEXA in its default configuration) use English as the pivot language $L_2$.

This raises a concern: if alignment is only measured against English, we cannot tell whether the model has a true interlingua specifically or just a good English to everything mapping. Evaluating with alternative pivots (French, German, Arabic, Chinese, Basque) is one way to test this. If alignment scores remain stable across pivots, the space is genuinely multilingual. If they drop for non-English pivots, the model's representations may be organized around English. We run this test in Section~\ref{sec:results-non-english-pivots}.

\section{Multilingual Evaluation}
\label{sec:multilingual-evaluation}

\subsection{Standard Benchmarks}
\label{subsec:standard-benchmarks}

Conventional multilingual evaluation measures downstream task accuracy on translated benchmarks, such as Belebele \citep{bandarkar2024belebele} for reading comprehension and m-ARC \citep{lai2023okapi} for reasoning. These benchmarks are useful for ranking models on functional performance, but they mix several factors: the quality of the model's internal representations, its generation ability, its sensitivity to prompt wording, and task-specific confounds. A model might score well not because its representations are aligned, but because it memorized surface patterns. Conversely, a model with strong internal alignment might underperform because of poor decoding.

\subsection{Reference-Based vs.\ Reference-Free Metrics}
\label{sec:ref-free-metrics}

Reference-based metrics (BLEU, chrF) evaluate generated translations against human references. They require external target outputs and only measure surface-level output quality. Reference-free metrics such as MEXA take a different approach: they inspect the transformer's hidden states directly from parallel sentence pairs, measuring how well the model's internal representations align across languages. It requires no text generation, task-specific fine-tuning or reference translations. This makes reference-free metrics faster to compute and easier to scale across hundreds of languages.

\subsection{Challenges in Alignment Evaluation}
\label{subsec:alignment-challenges}

Measuring alignment from hidden states is not straightforward. The main obstacle is representation \emph{anisotropy}: LLM embeddings tend to cluster in a narrow cone in vector space rather than spreading uniformly across all directions \citep{ethayarajh2019contextualized, hammerl2023anisotropy}. Figure~\ref{fig:anisotropy-illustration} illustrates the difference.

In an isotropic space, cosine similarity between unrelated sentence pairs is close to zero, so a high similarity between two sentences is a meaningful signal. In an anisotropic space, \emph{all} pairs have high cosine similarity simply because all vectors point in roughly the same direction. The similarity between a true translation pair may be 0.95, but the similarity between two unrelated sentences may also be 0.92. Raw cosine similarity cannot distinguish the two.

On top of anisotropy, each language has its own mean embedding offset (a ``language-identity vector''). Sentences in French cluster together and sentences in German cluster together, regardless of content. This offset inflates within-language similarity and makes cross-language similarity harder to interpret.

MEXA addresses both problems. The bidirectional retrieval criterion (Section~\ref{sec:mexa-pipeline}) does not rely on absolute cosine values; it only asks whether the true pair's similarity exceeds all distractors, which is a relative comparison unaffected by uniform shifts. The optional mean-centering step removes language-identity offsets entirely, isolating the semantic component of alignment.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/fig_anisotropy_illustration.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Isotropic vs.\ anisotropic embedding spaces}. Dimensions 1 and 2 depict a 2D projection (e.g., via principal components or latent coordinates $z_1, z_2$) of the high-dimensional representation space $\mathbb{R}^d$. (a) In an isotropic space, sentence embeddings disperse uniformly across directions, yielding near-zero cosine similarity for random sentence pairs. (b) In an anisotropic space (characteristic of deep LLMs), representations cluster within a narrow cone, causing high similarity across all pairs regardless of semantic alignment.}

\end{figure}

\section{Parallel Corpora}
\label{sec:parallel-corpora}

\subsection{Sources}
\label{subsec:parallel-sources}

All experiments in this thesis use two multi-parallel sentence datasets.

\paragraph{FLORES-200} \citep{nllbteam2022} is a high-quality benchmark derived from Wikipedia articles. It covers 200+ language-script pairs with professionally translated sentences on topics in geography, history, and science. The vocabulary is modern and syntactically varied, making it a good proxy for the kind of text LLMs encounter in their training data.

\paragraph{Super-Parallel Bible Corpus (sPBC)} \citep{mayer2014bible} contains verse-aligned Bible translations spanning over 1,400 language-script pairs. The text is historical and religious, with archaic vocabulary, repetitive semantic motifs, and unusual syntax. Despite its narrow domain, the Bible corpus is often the only parallel text available for extremely low-resource languages, including languages written in scripts absent from modern benchmarks (Ge'ez, Hebrew, various regional scripts).

The two corpora are complementary. FLORES tests alignment on modern, diverse prose; the Bible tests alignment on archaic, domain-restricted text. If a model's alignment rankings are consistent across both, the metric is measuring something stable about the model rather than something specific to the domain.

\subsection{Resource Disparity}
\label{subsec:resource-disparity}

Languages vary enormously in how much digital text exists for them. High-resource languages (English, French, German) have billions of tokens of web text and well-optimized tokenizers. Medium-resource languages (Russian, Hindi, Arabic, Chinese) have substantial but smaller corpora and less efficient tokenization. Low-resource languages (Amharic, Burmese, Khmer, Cherokee) may have only a few million tokens, leading to poor tokenizer coverage and high fertility.

Figure~\ref{fig:resource-disparity} shows the distribution of languages by script family across the two evaluation corpora. FLORES is dominated by Latin-script languages, while the Bible corpus extends much deeper into under-represented scripts. Using both corpora together gives evaluation coverage across the full resource spectrum.
