\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

This chapter describes the experimental setup. We first formalize the MEXA pipeline as implemented in our codebase (Section~\ref{sec:mexa-pipeline}), then describe the evaluated models (Section~\ref{sec:models}), the parallel corpora and their subsets (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}), and the experimental settings, analysis methods, and compute environment (Section~\ref{sec:experimental-settings}). The methodology follows \citet{kargaran2025mexa} for the reproduction experiments and extends it along four axes: new decoder families, mixture-of-experts architectures, encoder and embedding models, and non-English pivot languages.

\section{The MEXA Pipeline}
\label{sec:mexa-pipeline}

MEXA (Multilingual Evaluation via Cross-Lingual Alignment) estimates the multilingual coverage of a language model~$m$ from parallel sentences alone. We first describe the baseline four-stage evaluation pipeline as introduced by \citet{kargaran2025mexa} (Section~\ref{subsec:original-pipeline}), and then detail our proposed methodology extensions (Section~\ref{subsec:pipeline-extensions}).

% ..............................................
\subsection{Standard MEXA Pipeline}
\label{subsec:original-pipeline}

The reference MEXA framework operates in four sequential stages: sentence-embedding extraction, layer-wise similarity computation, alignment scoring, and aggregation, as schematically overviewed in Figure~\ref{fig:mexa-pipeline-diagram}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/fig_mexa_pipeline_diagram.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Schematic Overview of the MEXA Evaluation Pipeline}. The 4-stage pipeline extracts layer-wise hidden states, pools them with position weighting, computes the target-to-pivot similarity matrix $C^{(l)}$, applies bidirectional retrieval (P@1) filtering, and aggregates layer-wise scores into $\mu_{\text{Max}}$ and $\mu_{\text{Mean}}$.}
\label{fig:mexa-pipeline-diagram}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Stage 1: Layer-wise sentence embeddings.}
Let $\mathcal{S} = \{s_1, \dots, s_n\}$ be a set of $n$ parallel sentences available in a target language $L_1$ and a pivot language $L_2$ (English by default, but configurable). Each sentence $s_i$ is passed through the model, and hidden states $h_{i,t}^{(l)}$ are collected for every token $t \in \{1, \dots, T\}$ at each layer $l \in \{0, \dots, L\}$ (where $l=0$ represents the input embedding layer). For a given layer $l$, the token hidden states $\{h_{i,t}^{(l)}\}_{t=1}^T$ are reduced to a single sentence embedding $e_i^{(l)}$ using one of two strategies. The first is a position-weighted average, where tokens receive weights linearly proportional to their position index $t$—so that later tokens in the sequence receive larger weights than earlier ones—as shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted-avg}:
\begin{equation}
    e_i^{(l)} \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} w_t\, h_{i,t}^{(l)},
    \qquad
    w_t \;=\; \frac{t}{\sum_{k=1}^{T} k},
    \label{eq:weighted-avg}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the number of non-padding tokens. This is the primary setting in all reported results. The second is last-token pooling, which uses the hidden state of the final non-padding token at layer $l$, $e_i^{(l)} = h_{i,T}^{(l)}$. This position has attended to the entire sentence under causal attention, and is used as a robustness check. \citet{kargaran2025mexa} found that position-weighted average pooling consistently yields higher and more stable alignment scores than last-token pooling across causal decoders, as aggregating across all tokens mitigates sensitivity to single-token noise while preserving causal context accumulation.

For encoder-only models (Section~\ref{subsec:encoder-models}), whose attention is bidirectional, the same two reductions apply to the encoder hidden states, ensuring the pipeline is architecture-agnostic. Extracted embeddings are saved per language and per layer; consequently, alignment scoring (lightweight matrix calculations) is decoupled from embedding extraction (expensive GPU forward passes), enabling efficient re-evaluation of scoring variants without re-running model inference.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Similarity matrix.}
For each layer~$l \in \{0, \dots, L\}$ of language model~$m$, a pairwise cosine similarity matrix $C^{(l)} \equiv C(L_1, L_2, m, l) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is constructed across the full corpus of $n$ parallel sentences between target language $L_1$ and pivot language $L_2$, as shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:similarity-matrix}:
\begin{equation}
    C^{(l)} \;=\;
    \begin{pmatrix}
        c_{11}(l) & c_{12}(l) & \dots & c_{1n}(l) \\
        c_{21}(l) & c_{22}(l) & \dots & c_{2n}(l) \\
        \vdots    & \vdots    & \ddots & \vdots    \\
        c_{n1}(l) & c_{n2}(l) & \dots & c_{nn}(l)
    \end{pmatrix},
    \qquad
    c_{ij}(l) \;=\; \cos\!\left(e_{i, L_1}^{(l)},\, e_{j, L_2}^{(l)}\right),
    \label{eq:similarity-matrix}
\end{equation}
where entry $c_{ij}(l)$ denotes the cosine similarity between target sentence~$i \in L_1$ and pivot sentence~$j \in L_2$. The diagonal entries $c_{ii}(l)$ correspond to true parallel sentence pairs, while all off-diagonal entries $c_{ij}(l)$ ($i \neq j$) represent non-parallel distractors.

\paragraph{Stage 3: Alignment score.}
As discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:alignment-challenges}, absolute cosine values are unreliable in anisotropic LLM embedding spaces. MEXA therefore scores alignment with a \emph{bidirectional retrieval} (precision-at-1) criterion, as defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:mexa}: a parallel sentence pair~$(i, i)$ counts as aligned at layer~$l$ only if its true parallel similarity $c_{ii}(l)$ strictly exceeds every distractor entry in both its row ($c_{ij}(l)$, $j \neq i$) and its column ($c_{ji}(l)$, $j \neq i$),

\begin{equation}
    \mu C(L_1, L_2, m, l)
    \;=\;
    \frac{1}{n}
    \sum_{i=1}^{n}
    \mathbb{1}\!\left(
        c_{ii}(l) \;>\; \max_{j \neq i}\,\{\, c_{ij}(l),\; c_{ji}(l) \,\}
    \right).
    \label{eq:mexa}
\end{equation}

The score lies in $[0, 1]$. The expected value under random embeddings is approximately $1/n^2$ per pair (both the row and the column condition must hold by chance), so with $n = 100$ sentences a random baseline scores essentially zero. This validates the use of small parallel samples.

\paragraph{Stage 4: Aggregation across layers.}
The layer-wise scores are summarized into two model-level statistics, as shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:mexa-aggregation}:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{\text{Max}}(L_1) = \max_{l} \; \mu C(L_1, L_2, m, l),
    \qquad
    \mu_{\text{Mean}}(L_1) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{l=1}^{L} \mu C(L_1, L_2, m, l).
    \label{eq:mexa-aggregation}
\end{equation}
$\mu_{\text{Max}}$ captures the best alignment a model reaches at any depth, typically in the middle-to-late layers, consistent with the pivot hypothesis. $\mu_{\text{Mean}}$ rewards models that sustain alignment across many layers.

\subsection{Proposed Pipeline Extensions}
\label{subsec:pipeline-extensions}

To extend the baseline MEXA framework, we introduce two methodological extensions designed to isolate representation geometry and test structural pivot assumptions.

\paragraph{Mean-centered alignment scoring.}
To separate semantic alignment from the language identity signal (Section~\ref{subsec:alignment-challenges}), we propose a centered variant of Equation~\eqref{eq:mexa}. This extension is inspired by \citet{schuster2019crosslingual}, who demonstrated that multilingual contextual representations contain a dominant language-specific centroid offset that can artificially separate language clusters at the expense of semantic alignment.

Before building the similarity matrix at layer~$l$, each language's embeddings are shifted by their sample mean vector, yielding the centered sentence embedding $\tilde{e}_i^{(l)}$ defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:mean-centering}:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{e}_i^{(l)} \;=\; e_i^{(l)} - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} e_k^{(l)},
    \label{eq:mean-centering}
\end{equation}
applied independently to the target language $L_1$ and pivot language $L_2$. As illustrated geometrically in Figure~\ref{fig:meancentering-geometry} via a two-dimensional Principal Component Analysis (PCA) projection of the sentence embedding space, subtracting language-specific means centers both vector manifolds at the origin, eliminating the dominant language-identity vector $\bar{e}_{L}$ shared by all sentences of a language and sharpening semantic retrieval. Comparing centered alignment scores computed via Equation~\eqref{eq:mean-centering} against uncentered scores from Equation~\eqref{eq:mexa} quantifies how much of the measured alignment is carried by language-mean offsets rather than sentence-level semantics.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/fig_meancentering_geometry.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Vector Space Geometry of Mean Centering}. 2D Principal Component Analysis (PCA) projection of high-dimensional sentence embeddings. (a) Uncentered sentence embeddings projected onto the first two principal components (PC1, PC2) are offset into distinct language clusters by a dominant language-identity vector $\bar{e}_{L}$. (b) Subtracting language means centers both manifolds at the origin, eliminating language identity bias and isolating semantic alignment.}
\label{fig:meancentering-geometry}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Non-English pivot substitution.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:mexa} does not privilege English mathematically; the pivot $L_2$ is simply a parameter. We propose extending the pipeline to test the pivot hypothesis directly by recomputing all alignment scores across five diverse non-English pivot languages: high-resource Latin-script (French, German), non-Latin-script (Arabic, Chinese), and a language isolate (Basque). Both standard and centered variants are evaluated.

\subsection{Reproduction Models}
\label{subsec:reproduction-models}

To validate our implementation against \citet{kargaran2025mexa}, we re-evaluate two models that have published reference scores: \textbf{Llama~3.1~8B} \citep{dubey2024llama3}, the primary English-centric decoder in the original paper, and \textbf{Mistral~7B~v0.3} \citep{jiang2023mistral}. If our $\mu_{\text{Max}}$/$\mu_{\text{Mean}}$ values agree with the published ones on the reference benchmark configurations (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}), the embedding extraction and scoring code can be considered correct, and the extended experiments can proceed on a verified foundation.

\subsection{New Decoder Families}
\label{subsec:new-decoders}

The first extension applies MEXA to decoder families released after the original study.

Qwen~3 \citep{yang2025qwen3}, evaluated on four parameter sizes (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, and 8B), was trained on a more multilingual data mixture than Llama. The size sweep isolates the effect of scale on alignment. Qwen~3.5~9B represents the successor generation and allows measuring recipe-level progress at a roughly constant parameter count. Apertus \citep{apertus2025}, evaluated across four checkpoints (\textbf{Apertus-Mini~4B}, \textbf{Apertus~8B}, \textbf{Apertus~1.5~8B}, and \textbf{Apertus~1.5~70B}), comprises fully-open multilingual models trained under an explicitly compliance-focused data policy, serving as a counterpoint to English-centric pre-training. Gemma~4 \citep{gemma2024gemma} spans four dense variants (\textbf{E2B}, \textbf{E4B}, \textbf{12B}, and \textbf{31B}); here, the \textit{E} designation denotes Edge-optimized architectures (\textbf{E2B} and \textbf{E4B}) engineered for lightweight deployment on mobile and edge devices, while \textbf{12B} and \textbf{31B} represent high-capacity dense decoders for desktop and server workloads.

\noindent
Together with the reproduction models, these give us size ladders within dense families (Qwen3 0.6B$\to$8B, Gemma~4 E2B$\to$31B) and recipe contrasts across families at matched scale (Llama~3.1~8B vs.\ Qwen3~8B vs.\ Apertus~8B). Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models within these families (such as \textbf{Gemma~4~26B-A4B} and the \textbf{Mixtral} series) are presented separately in Section~\ref{subsec:moe-models} below.

% ..............................................
\subsection{Mixture-of-Experts Models}
\label{subsec:moe-models}

Sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures route each token through a small subset of expert feed-forward networks, so total parameter capacity and per-token compute are decoupled \citep{jiang2024mixtral}. To isolate the specific impact of expert routing compared to dense decoders (Section~\ref{subsec:new-decoders}), we evaluate sparse MoE models as a distinct category. Whether expert routing helps or hurts cross-lingual alignment is an open question: experts could specialize by language, which would fragment the shared space, or by function, which would leave alignment intact.

We evaluate \textbf{Mixtral~8x7B} and \textbf{Mixtral~8x22B} \citep{jiang2024mixtral} (top-2 routing across 8 experts) against the dense Mistral~7B~v0.3, as well as \textbf{Gemma~4~26B-A4B} \citep{gemma2024gemma} against dense Gemma~4 variants. In \textbf{Gemma~4~26B-A4B}, the \textit{A4B} notation specifies that out of 26 billion total parameters, only 4 billion parameters are active per token during inference, directly isolating the effect of sparse conditional routing on representation geometry. The MEXA pipeline applies unchanged in all cases, since it only consumes post-layer hidden states.

\subsection{Encoder and Embedding Models}
\label{subsec:encoder-models}

The final model category contrasts causal decoders with bidirectional encoders and dedicated sentence-embedding architectures. While cross-lingual alignment in decoders is an implicit secondary outcome of next-token prediction, sentence-embedding models train explicitly with contrastive metric-learning objectives, providing an empirical upper bound for alignment.

We evaluate three sub-categories:

\paragraph{Masked-LM Encoders.}
To test whether bidirectional self-attention yields stronger alignment than causal decoders, we evaluate \textbf{XLM-RoBERTa Base} (270M parameters) and \textbf{XLM-RoBERTa Large} (550M parameters) \citep{conneau2020xlmr}, pre-trained on 2.5TB of 100-language CommonCrawl data via Masked Language Modeling (MLM). We also evaluate \textbf{Glot500} (270M parameters) \citep{imani2023glot500}, extending XLM-R to 500+ languages for low-resource Bible coverage, and \textbf{mmBERT-base} (270M parameters) \citep{mmbert2025}.

\paragraph{Contrastive Sentence Encoders.}
To measure the alignment ceiling under explicit metric learning, we evaluate dual-encoders optimized with translation ranking losses: \textbf{LaBSE} (471M parameters) \citep{feng2022labse} and \textbf{multilingual-E5-base} (278M parameters) \citep{wang2024multilingual}.

\paragraph{Decoder-based Embedding Models.}
To isolate the effect of contrastive fine-tuning on the exact same architecture, we evaluate the \textbf{Qwen3-Embedding} series \citep{yang2025qwen3} (0.6B, 4B, and 8B parameters), which fine-tunes the Qwen3 causal backbone (Section~\ref{subsec:new-decoders}) for retrieval.

\paragraph{FLORES-200.}
FLORES-200\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/facebook/flores}} \citep{nllbteam2022} is Meta's benchmark dataset designed to evaluate multilingual machine translation and representation quality. Crucially, all parallel sentences in FLORES-200 are professionally \textbf{human-translated} from English Wikipedia articles rather than machine-translated, ensuring high translation fidelity, natural phrasing, and sentence structures free of automated translation artifacts.

Throughout this thesis, we carefully distinguish among five distinct levels of linguistic grouping:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Language families:} Broad genealogical groupings sharing ancestral origin (e.g., Indo-European, Afroasiatic, Sino-Tibetan, Niger-Congo).
    \item \textbf{Individual languages:} Linguistic entities defined by standard ISO 639-3 identifiers (such as Serbian, Kurdish, Chinese, or English).
    \item \textbf{Language-script pairs (varieties):} Specific combinations of a language and an orthographic writing system. Although the benchmark is named ``FLORES-200'', it formally contains \textbf{204 language-script pairs} covering approximately 200 distinct languages. Distinguishing language-script pairs from raw languages is essential because several languages are represented in multiple orthographies (e.g., Serbian in Cyrillic \texttt{srp\_Cyrl} and Latin \texttt{srp\_Latn}; Chinese in Simplified \texttt{zho\_Hans} and Traditional \texttt{zho\_Hant}; Kurdish in Kurmanji Latin \texttt{kmr\_Latn} and Sorani Arabic \texttt{ckb\_Arab}).
    \item \textbf{Filtered benchmark evaluation pairs (Belebele overlap):} To enable direct reproduction and comparison with \citet{kargaran2025mexa}, we evaluate the subset of \textbf{116 language-script pairs} that directly overlap with the Belebele reading comprehension benchmark \citep{bandarkar2024belebele}.
    \item \textbf{Cross-lingual target pairs vs.\ English pivot:} When English (\texttt{eng\_Latn}) serves as the reference pivot language $L_2$, evaluating the full 204 language-script inventory yields \textbf{203 cross-lingual target pairs} ($L_1 \neq L_2$).
\end{enumerate}

From the FLORES-200 devtest split, we evaluate three primary configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FLORES Benchmark subset (116 pairs):} Evaluates the 116 Belebele-overlapping language-script pairs at $n = 100$ parallel sentences per language. This matches the standard reference setup used for reproduction and cross-model ranking.
    \item \textbf{FLORES (1012) subset (116 pairs):} Uses the same 116 Belebele-overlapping language-script pairs but scales sentence depth to all $n = 1{,}012$ devtest sentences, isolating the effect of distractor density on retrieval precision while holding language coverage constant.
    \item \textbf{FLORES Full (204 pairs / 203 target pairs):} Encompasses the complete set of 204 language-script pairs at $n = 1{,}012$ sentences (evaluating 203 non-English target varieties against the English pivot) for maximal typological coverage.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Super-Parallel Bible Corpus (sPBC).}
From the Super-Parallel Bible Corpus (sPBC) \citep{mayer2014bible}, we evaluate two configurations. The Bible benchmark subset contains 101 languages with $n = 103$ parallel sentences, matching the original paper's setup. The Bible Full dataset covers the complete corpus of 1{,}401 language-script pairs, extending evaluation deep into the low-resource tail.

Unlike the modern encyclopedic prose of FLORES, the sPBC is entirely historical and religious, characterized by archaic vocabulary, repetitive semantic motifs, and distinctive grammatical structures. However, as a massively multi-way parallel corpus covering 1{,}401 language-script pairs, it provides unparalleled linguistic coverage. This includes historical scripts and orthographies absent from modern web-crawled benchmarks such as Syriac, Coptic, and Ge'ez (Ethiopic) as well as indigenous writing systems. For hundreds of extremely low-resource languages that lack digital web footprints or Wikipedia entries, the Bible corpus represents the sole available multi-way parallel resource for cross-lingual NLP evaluation.

English (\texttt{eng\_Latn}) is the pivot in all standard runs. The pivot-substitution experiments of Section~\ref{sec:mexa-pipeline} use the FLORES benchmark subset.

\section{Experimental Settings}
\label{sec:experimental-settings}

% ..............................................
\subsection{Evaluation and Analysis Methods}
\label{subsec:analysis-methods}

Unless stated otherwise, reported scores use position-weighted average embeddings (Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted-avg}) and are given as $\mu_{\text{Max}}$ and $\mu_{\text{Mean}}$ per model-dataset configuration; last-token embeddings and the centered variant are reported as ablations.

The analysis operates across four hierarchical levels: (1)~model level, (2)~language level, (3)~layer level, and (4)~validity level. At the model level, scores are averaged over all languages of a configuration for family and scale comparisons and for the reproduction check against published values. At the language level, per-language scores are ranked and grouped by script and resource level, then cross-referenced with tokenizer fertility (computed on both corpora) to separate tokenization effects from representational ones. At the layer level, full layer-wise alignment profiles $\mu C(\cdot, l)$ are examined to locate where in the network alignment peaks and to test the intermediate-layer prediction of the pivot hypothesis. At the validity level, Pearson correlations between MEXA scores and downstream performance on Belebele \citep{bandarkar2024belebele} (across 116 overlapping languages) and m-ARC \citep{lai2023okapi} (across 30 overlapping languages) are computed to test whether alignment predicts task performance. Cross-model correlations measuring consistency across architectures are also evaluated.

For qualitative inspection, sentence embeddings are projected to two and three dimensions with Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and t-SNE \citep{van2008visualizing}, colored by language and script, to visualize the clustering behavior that the retrieval score summarizes numerically.

% ..............................................
\subsection{Implementation and Compute}
\label{subsec:implementation}

The evaluation pipeline is implemented in Python using PyTorch and Hugging Face Transformers. The embedding extractor loads each checkpoint via \texttt{AutoModelForCausalLM} (or \texttt{AutoModel} for encoder models) with all hidden states enabled, tokenizes each sentence using the model's native tokenizer, and serializes per-layer sentence embeddings to disk as NumPy arrays per language. The scoring module then computes the layer-wise cosine similarity matrices and evaluates the cross-lingual retrieval accuracy defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:mexa}.

Formatted per-language and per-layer scores are exported to CSV and JSON formats and explored interactively using a custom React dashboard developed for this thesis.

Lightweight models under 3B parameters (such as XLM-R Base/Large, Glot500, mmBERT, LaBSE, mE5-base, and Qwen3 0.6B/1.7B) were evaluated on local workstation GPUs. Large-scale extraction jobs (8B-parameter decoders, Gemma~4 31B, Mixture-of-Experts models like Mixtral 8x22B, and full 1,400-language Bible sweeps) were executed on the COMA cluster equipped with NVIDIA H200 (141GB) and H100 (80GB) GPUs. Embedding extraction and scoring required approximately 15 to 25 minutes per 8B-parameter model sweep over FLORES-200 (116 languages, $n=100$), totaling approximately 120 GPU hours across the complete experimental matrix.
