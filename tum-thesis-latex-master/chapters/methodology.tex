\section{The MEXA Pipeline}
\label{sec:mexa-pipeline}

MEXA (Multilingual Evaluation via Cross-Lingual Alignment) estimates the
multilingual coverage of a language model $m$ from parallel sentences
alone, in four stages: sentence-embedding extraction, layer-wise
similarity computation, alignment scoring, and aggregation.

\paragraph{Stage 1: Layer-wise sentence embeddings.}
Let $\mathcal{S} = \{s_1, \dots, s_n\}$ be a set of $n$ parallel sentences
available in a target language $L_1$ and a pivot language $L_2$ (English and can be cha nged ). Each sentence is passed through the model, and
hidden states $h_t^{(l)}$ are collected for every token $t$ at every layer
$l \in \{1, \dots, L\}$, including the embedding layer. Token states are
reduced to a single sentence embedding $e^{(l)}$ per layer with one of two strategies: first, a position-weighted average, where tokens are averaged with weights proportional to their position to account for the fact that early tokens under causal attention see little context while late tokens see all of it:
\begin{equation}
    e^{(l)} \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} w_t\, h_t^{(l)},
    \qquad
    w_t \;=\; \frac{t}{\sum_{k=1}^{T} k},
    \label{eq:weighted-avg}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the number of non-padding tokens (this is the primary setting in all reported results) and last token pooling, which uses the hidden state of the final non-padding token, $e^{(l)} = h_T^{(l)}$, representing the only position that has attended to the entire sentence under causal attention (this is used as a robustness check).

For encoder-only models (Section~\ref{subsec:encoder-models}), whose
attention is bidirectional, the same two reductions are applied to the
encoder hidden states, making the pipeline architecture-agnostic.
Embeddings are stored per language and per layer, so that scoring is
decoupled from (and far cheaper than) extraction.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Similarity matrix.}
For each layer $l$, a cosine similarity matrix
$C(L_1, L_2, m, l) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is computed between the
$n$ target-language embeddings and the $n$ pivot-language embeddings:
$c_{ij}(l)$ is the cosine similarity between target sentence $i$ and pivot
sentence $j$. The diagonal entries $c_{ii}(l)$ correspond to true parallel
pairs; all off-diagonal entries are non-parallel distractors.

\paragraph{Stage 3: Alignment score.}
As discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:alignment-challenges}, absolute cosine
values are unreliable in anisotropic LLM embedding spaces. MEXA therefore
scores alignment with a \emph{bidirectional retrieval} (precision-at-1)
criterion: a sentence pair counts as aligned only if its true similarity
strictly exceeds every distractor in both its row and its column,

\begin{equation}
    \mu C(L_1, L_2, m, l)
    \;=\;
    \frac{1}{n}
    \sum_{i=1}^{n}
    \mathbb{1}\!\left(
        c_{ii}(l) \;>\; \max_{j \neq i}\,\{\, c_{ij}(l),\; c_{ji}(l) \,\}
    \right).
    \label{eq:mexa}
\end{equation}

The score lies in $[0, 1]$ the expected value under random embeddings is
approximately $1/n^2$ per pair (both the row and the column condition must
hold by chance), so with $n = 100$ sentences a random baseline scores
essentially zero, which validates the use of small parallel samples.

\paragraph{Stage 4: Aggregation across layers.}
The layer-wise scores are summarized into two model-level statistics:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{\text{Max}}(L_1) = \max_{l} \; \mu C(L_1, L_2, m, l),
    \qquad
    \mu_{\text{Mean}}(L_1) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{l=1}^{L} \mu C(L_1, L_2, m, l).
\end{equation}
$\mu_{\text{Max}}$ captures the peak alignment a model achieves anywhere
in its depth in the middle-to-late layers, which is consistent with
the pivot hypothesis and $\mu_{\text{Mean}}$ rewards models that
sustain alignment across many layers.

\paragraph{Extension: mean-centered scoring.}
To separate semantic alignment from the language identity signal
(Section~\ref{subsec:alignment-challenges}), we additionally compute a centered variant of Equation~\eqref{eq:mexa}: before building the similarity matrix at layer $l$, each language's embeddings are shifted by
their own mean
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{e}_i^{(l)} \;=\; e_i^{(l)} - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} e_k^{(l)},
\end{equation}
applied  to the target and pivot sides. Centering removes the
dominant direction shared by all sentences of a language and 
sharpens retrieval; comparing centered and uncentered scores quantifies
how much measured alignment is carried by language mean offsets rather
than sentence level semantics.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/fig_meancentering_geometry.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Vector Space Geometry of Mean Centering}. (a) Uncentered sentence embeddings are offset into distinct language clusters by a dominant language-identity vector $\bar{e}_{L}$. (b) Subtracting language means centers both manifolds at the origin, eliminating language identity bias and isolating semantic alignment.}
\label{fig:meancentering-geometry}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Extension: non-English pivots.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:mexa} does not privilege English mathematically; the
pivot $L_2$ is a parameter that we can change. We exploit this to test the pivot
hypothesis directly, recomputing all alignment scores with French, German,
Arabic, Chinese and Basque as pivot languages spanning high-resource
Latin-script, non-Latin-script, and a language isolate in both the
standard and centered variants.

\subsection{Reproduction Models}
\label{subsec:reproduction-models}

To validate our implementation against \citet{kargaran2025mexa}, we
re-evaluate two models with published reference scores:
\textbf{Llama~3.1~8B} \citep{dubey2024llama3}, the primary English-centric
decoder studied in the original paper and \textbf{Mistral~7B~v0.3}
\citep{jiang2023mistral}. Agreement with the published
$\mu_{\text{Max}}$/$\mu_{\text{Mean}}$ values on the reference benchmark
configurations (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}) establishes that our
embedding extraction and scoring code reproduces the original pipeline
and enable the extensions of experiments .

% ..............................................
\subsection{New Decoder Families}
\label{subsec:new-decoders}

The first extension applies MEXA to decoder families released after the original study, covering different data mixtures and scales. First, Qwen3 \citep{yang2025qwen3}, at 0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, and 8B parameters, represents a family trained with a more multilingual data mixture than Llama, where the size sweep isolates the effect of scale. Second, Qwen3.5 9B represents the successor generation, used to measure recipe-level progress at approximately constant parameter count. Third, Apertus 8B and Apertus-Mini 4B are openly trained models with an explicitly multilingual, compliance-focused data policy, providing a counterpoint to English-centric training. Fourth, Gemma 4 (spanning five variants: E2B, E4B, 12B, 31B, and the sparse 26B-A4B) is Google's open-weight family, spanning efficient edge-scale to 31B-parameter models.

\noindent
Together with the reproduction models, this yields size ladders within
families (Qwen3 0.6B$\to$8B, Gemma~4 E2B$\to$31B) and recipe contrasts
across families at matched scale (Llama~3.1~8B vs.\ Qwen3~8B vs.\
Apertus~8B).

% ..............................................
\subsection{Mixture-of-Experts Models}
\label{subsec:moe-models}

Sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures route each token through a
small subset of expert feed-forward networks, decoupling parameter count
from per-token compute \citep{jiang2024mixtral}. Whether expert routing helps or hurts cross-lingual alignment is an open question: 
experts could specialize by language (fragmenting the shared space) or by function (leaving alignment intact). 

We evaluate \textbf{Mixtral~8x7B} and \textbf{Mixtral~8x22B} \citep{jiang2024mixtral}, comparing them against
the dense  Mistral~7B~v0.3, and \textbf{Gemma~4~26B-A4B},
comparing it against dense Gemma~4 variants. In all cases the MEXA
pipeline applies unchanged, since it only consumes the post-layer hidden states.

% ..............................................
\subsection{Encoder and Embedding Models}
\label{subsec:encoder-models}

The final model axis contrasts causal decoders with bidirectional
encoders and purpose-built sentence-embedding models, which provide an
upper-bound reference for what alignment looks like when it is an explicit
training objective rather than a by-product. We evaluate three sub-categories. First, Masked-LM encoders include XLM-R base and large \citep{conneau2020xlmr}, Glot500 \citep{imani2023glot500} (an XLM-R variant extended to 500+ languages, relevant for the Bible corpus tail), and mmBERT-base. Second, contrastively trained sentence encoders include LaBSE \citep{feng2022labse} and multilingual-E5-base \citep{wang2024multilingual}, both explicitly optimized so that translations are nearest neighbors. Third, decoder-based embedding models include the Qwen3-Embedding series at 0.6B, 4B, and 8B, which share the Qwen3 architecture but are fine-tuned for retrieval, allowing a controlled comparison of the same architecture before and after embedding-specific training.

\paragraph{FLORES-200.}
From the FLORES-200 devtest split \citep{nllbteam2022} we use three main configurations: first, the FLORES benchmark subset, containing the 116 languages overlapping with Belebele, with $n = 100$ parallel sentences per language (the exact benchmark configuration in \citet{kargaran2025mexa}, used for reproduction and for all model-family comparisons); second, FLORES (1012), which uses the same 116 languages with all $n = 1{,}012$ devtest sentences to quantify the effect of sample size on the retrieval criterion (larger $n$ means more distractors and therefore a strictly harder task); and third, FLORES Full, which includes all 203 language script pairs for maximal high-quality coverage.

\paragraph{Parallel Bible Corpus (sPBC).}
From the super-parallel Bible corpus \citep{mayer2014bible} we use two main configurations: first, the Bible benchmark subset, containing 101 languages with $n = 103$ parallel sentences, matching the original paper's Bible configuration; and second, the Bible Full dataset, covering the complete corpus of more than 1{,}400 language script pairs, which extends evaluation far into the low-resource tail.

English (\texttt{eng\_Latn}) serves as the pivot in all standard runs; the
pivot-substitution runs of Section~\ref{sec:mexa-pipeline} use the FLORES
benchmark subset.

\subsection{Evaluation and Analysis Methods}
\label{subsec:analysis-methods}

Unless stated otherwise, reported scores use position-weighted average
embeddings (Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted-avg}) and are given as
$\mu_{\text{Max}}$ and $\mu_{\text{Mean}}$ per model dataset
configuration; last-token embeddings and the centered variant are reported as ablations. The analysis is conducted on four levels. First, at the model level, scores are averaged over all languages of a configuration, enabling family and scale comparisons and the reproduction check against published values. Second, at the language level, per-language scores are ranked and grouped by script and by resource level, and cross-referenced with tokenizer fertility (computed on both corpora) to separate tokenization effects from representational effects. Third, at the layer level, full layer-wise alignment profiles $\mu C(\cdot, l)$ are analyzed to locate where in the network alignment peaks and to test the intermediate-layer prediction of the pivot hypothesis. Fourth, at the validity level, Pearson correlations between MEXA scores and downstream accuracy on Belebele \citep{bandarkar2024belebele} and m-ARC \citep{lai2023okapi} over the languages both cover are evaluated to test whether alignment enhances task performance, alongside cross-model correlations measuring consistency across architectures.

For qualitative inspection, sentence embeddings are also projected to two and three dimensions with PCA and t-SNE, colored by language and script,
to visualize the clustering behavior that the retrieval score summarizes
numerically.

\subsection{Implementation and Compute}
\label{subsec:implementation}

The pipeline is implemented in Python with PyTorch and Hugging Face
Transformers. \texttt{embed\_extractor.py} loads each checkpoint via
\texttt{AutoModelForCausalLM} (or \texttt{AutoModel} for encoders) with
output of all hidden states enabled, tokenizes each sentence with the
model's own tokenizer and stores per-layer sentence embeddings as
serialized NumPy arrays, one file per language.
\texttt{compute\_mexa.py} then builds the layer-wise cosine similarity
matrices in  and evaluates Equation~\eqref{eq:mexa}; 

Formatted per-language and per-layer scores are
exported to CSV/JSON and explored in an interactive React dashboard developed for this thesis.

Small models were run locally; all large-scale extraction jobs (8B and
above, MoE and full-corpus sweeps) were executed on the CIT-TUM-HN
SLURM cluster at TUM Campus Heilbronn on NVIDIA data-center GPUs,
